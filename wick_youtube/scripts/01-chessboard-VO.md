# Episode 01: The Chessboard That Bankrupted a King
## Voiceover script (read this aloud)

**How to record:** read at a relaxed pace (about 150 words a minute). **Leave a clear one-second pause between numbered lines.** The engine finds those pauses and times every animation to them, so the gaps matter more than a perfect take. Record in a quiet room with your phone about a hand-width from your mouth. Separate takes per section are fine.

**Say it like this:** Ibn Khallikan = "IB-in kha-LEE-kan". "Quintillion" = "kwin-TIL-yun".


### COLD OPEN

**B01.** A single grain of wheat sits on the corner square of a chessboard. By the last square, that one grain will have grown into more wheat than the whole world grows in about a thousand years.

**B02.** The same math is quietly running on your money right now. And it cares about one thing even more than how much you have. It cares about when you started.

**B03.** By the end, you'll see how ten years of doing a little can beat thirty years of doing a lot. But first, a king has to lose a bet.


### SETUP · The Inventor's Price

**B04.** Nobody knows if this story really happened. One of the oldest written versions comes from a scholar named Ibn Khallikan, in the year twelve fifty six. The legend was old even then.

**B05.** It goes like this. A clever man invents the game of chess. The king loves it so much that he offers the inventor any reward he wants. Gold. Land. A palace.

**B06.** The inventor waves it all away. He asks for grain. One grain on the first square. Two on the second. Four on the third. Double it on every square, all sixty four of them.

**B07.** The king laughs. He thinks the inventor is a fool. He tells his treasurer to fetch a small bag of wheat and be done with it.

**B08.** The king's mistake is the most human mistake there is. He did the math the way our brains like to do it. By adding. But the board wasn't adding. It was multiplying.


### CH1 · The First Squares

**B09.** At first, the king looks right. The tenth square needs five hundred and twelve grains. Add up the first ten squares and it's barely a thousand. A handful.

**B10.** That's what doubling looks like at the start. It looks like nothing. Each new square adds as much as everything before it. And when everything before it is tiny, the new square is tiny too.

**B11.** Then the numbers start to move. By square twenty one, the treasurer needs over a million grains for that one square alone. Sacks now. Then carts.

**B12.** By square thirty two, halfway across the board, the king owes about four billion grains in total. That's a couple of hundred tonnes of wheat. A few barns. Painful, but a kingdom can pay it. So here is the question the king never thought to ask. What happens on the other half?


### CH2 · The Other Half

**B13.** Every square on the second half holds more than the entire first half put together. The very last square holds more grain than all sixty three squares before it, combined.

**B14.** Add it all up and the king owes about eighteen quintillion grains. That's an eighteen followed by eighteen zeros.

**B15.** How much is that? A grain of wheat weighs a tiny fraction of a gram. Even if you pick a small grain, the whole pile would weigh over seven hundred billion tonnes.

**B16.** The whole world today grows a little over eight hundred million tonnes of wheat a year. At that pace, paying the inventor would take roughly a thousand years of every wheat field on Earth.

**B17.** The legend ends differently depending on who tells it. In some versions, the king makes the inventor his advisor. In others, he has him killed, which is one way to settle a debt.

**B18.** Either way, the lesson is the same. The first half of the board looked like nothing. All the power lived in the second half. So what does the board look like when the grains are dollars?


### CH3 · Wick's Chessboard

**B19.** Meet Wick at twenty five. He sets up an automatic transfer of two hundred dollars a month into a stock index fund. That's one basket that holds hundreds of big companies at once. He doesn't pick stocks. He doesn't watch the news.

**B20.** Here's how it grows. Those companies earn money, and they grow in value. That growth gets added to Wick's pile. Next year, the growth earns growth of its own. That's compounding. Earning returns on your returns.

**B21.** For the math, let's assume seven percent a year after inflation. That's about what the American stock market has averaged since nineteen twenty eight. It's an average, not a promise. In some single years, the market fell by more than a third.

**B22.** When the market drops, Wick's balance drops too. He keeps sending the two hundred anyway. Lower prices just mean his next two hundred buys a slightly bigger piece of the basket.

**B23.** After one year, Wick has put in two thousand four hundred dollars. And his growth? About seventy six dollars. That's the first square. He could have bought a nice dinner with it. It looks like nothing because it nearly is nothing.

**B24.** After ten years, he's put in twenty four thousand. Growth has added about ten thousand more. Nice. Not life changing. Most people quit somewhere in here. Not because it failed. Because it's boring.

**B25.** But Wick keeps going. By sixty five, forty years in, he's put in ninety six thousand dollars. His account holds about four hundred and ninety four thousand. About four of every five dollars came from growth, not from him.

**B26.** That's his chessboard. But there's a second Wick in this story. And he's about to learn what the king learned.


### CH4 · The Late Start

**B27.** This Wick waits. He figures he'll start when he earns more. At thirty five, he begins the same two hundred dollars a month. Same fund, same seven percent, all the way to sixty five.

**B28.** He puts in seventy two thousand dollars. He ends with about two hundred and thirty four thousand. Starting ten years late cut his result by more than half.

**B29.** To catch up, the late Wick would need to invest about four hundred and twenty three dollars every month. More than double. Same finish line. Twice the effort. All because he skipped the start.

**B30.** He didn't really lose the first ten years. He lost the last ten. The squares at the end of the board, where each one is bigger than all the others. Those are the years you can never make up. So why does almost everyone start late?


### THE TURN

**B31.** Here's the part that trips everyone up. The king didn't lose because he was stupid. He lost because the first squares looked like nothing. And that is exactly why people quit.

**B32.** Think about what the first years actually feel like. You give up real things. A dinner out. A weekend trip. And the account barely moves. So your brain does what the king's did. It looks at the squares it can see, and it laughs.

**B33.** Compounding does its best work at the end. But it only reaches the end if you give it the beginning. You can earn more money later. You can save harder later. You can't buy back the years.

**B34.** Time is the one ingredient you can't buy later.


### PAYOFF

**B35.** So here's the answer to the question from the start. Picture a third Wick. He invests two hundred a month from twenty five to thirty five. Ten years. Then he stops for good and never adds another dollar.

**B36.** The hammock Wick puts in twenty four thousand dollars. The late Wick puts in seventy two thousand. At sixty five, the one who stopped has about two hundred and sixty thousand. The one who started late has about two hundred and thirty four.

**B37.** Ten years of doing a little beat thirty years of doing a lot. Not because he was smarter. Because his money reached the second half of the board.

**B38.** And notice what the hammock Wick didn't need. He didn't need a bigger salary, a hot stock tip, or perfect timing. He needed ten boring years that started early.


### FIRST STEP + CLOSE

**B39.** So here's one thing you can do tonight. Set up one small automatic transfer into savings or investing, timed for the day you get paid. Any amount. The amount can grow later. The start date can't move.

**B40.** The king laughed at one grain of wheat. The grain didn't mind. It had time.
