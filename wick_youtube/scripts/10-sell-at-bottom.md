# Why Everyone Sells at the Bottom

**Logline:** Why crashes happen, why an ancient alarm in our heads makes us sell at the worst possible moment, and why the market's best days hide right beside its worst.

**The loop:** Twenty years of returns hinge on about ten days. Why do those days sit right next to the worst ones, and why does almost everyone miss them the same way?

**The turn:** The market moves money from the impatient to the patient.

## CH2 · The Tulip Truth

**B07** · `TIMELINE` · music: history
VO: People have felt this for a very long time. The most famous case is the Dutch tulip mania. By early sixteen thirty seven, rare tulip bulbs were selling for startling prices. Then, in the first week of February, came a sale in the city of Haarlem. Bulbs were offered. Nobody bid.
VISUAL: Switch to PARCHMENT palette. A timeline scrolls right to left from 2025 back through the centuries, years ticking past, and brakes on "FEB 1637". ON-SCREEN: Haarlem, February 1637

**B08** · `QUOTE` · music: history

VISUAL: A flat silhouette portrait of a Victorian man with sideburns, labeled "Charles Mackay, 1841". **B09** · `STAGE` · music: history
VO: Then a historian named Anne Goldgar went looking for those ruined merchants in the Dutch archives. She could not find one bankruptcy she could clearly blame on tulips. The trade was mostly among well off merchants and skilled craftsmen. And the wider Dutch economy carried on.
VISUAL: Wick, curious, in a tall flat archive with rolling ladders, wearing a small scholar's cape below the neck. He blows dust off a ledger, opens it, finds a nearly blank page with one small stamp: "NO TULIP BANKRUPTS". He opens another. Same stamp. **B10** · `SPLIT` · music: history

VISUAL: Split screen. Left panel titled THE LEGEND: flames, crying crowds, a canal. **B11** · `CROWD` · music: history

VISUAL: A town square of about fifty tiny candle figures. One in the middle glances nervously left; the neighbor glances too; the glance ripples outward like a wave until the whole crowd turns and hurries off in the same direction. One bulb is left alone on the cobblestones.
ON-SCREEN: Fear spreads faster than facts.

## CH3 · Twice the Pain

**B12** · `QUOTE` · music: wonder
VO: In nineteen seventy nine, two psychologists published a paper on how people really choose when outcomes are uncertain. Their names were Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky. One idea from it became famous. Losses loom larger than gains.
VISUAL: Back to PAPER palette. Two flat portrait silhouettes side by side, labeled Kahneman and Tversky, with a journal cover behind them reading "Econometrica, 1979". The quote writes on in marker between them.
ON-SCREEN: "Losses loom larger than gains."

**B13** · `CHART_BAR` · music: wonder
VO: Their later work put a rough number on it. A loss feels about twice as strong as a gain of the same size. So losing a hundred dollars stings about as much as winning two hundred feels good. The scale in your head is not balanced.
VISUAL: A flat balance scale. A green bar labeled "WIN $100" drops onto the left pan; the scale barely moves. A red bar labeled "LOSE $100" drops onto the right pan and slams it down with a thud. A second green "WIN $100" lands on the left, and only then does the scale level out. Wick stands beside it, focused, tapping the beam with one finger.
ON-SCREEN: Lose $100 feels like Win $200
SFX: thud

**B14** · `STAGE` · music: wonder
VO: One common explanation goes back to survival. For most of human history, a windfall was nice. But losing your food store could mean not making it through the winter. So the alarm for loss was built loud.
VISUAL: Wick in a flat cave with a small fire, a fur wrap below the neck, calm, guarding a little pile of berries. A wolf's shadow slides across the cave mouth. Wick, alarmed, hugs the berries to his chest. On the cave wall, a charcoal scratching reads "LOSSES: BAD. BERRIES: GOOD."

**B15** · `SPLIT` · music: tension
VO: Now put that old alarm in front of a stock chart. Prices go up and down every day. An alarm that fires twice as hard makes the down days feel huge. The up days feel small. Check your balance every morning, and the market starts to feel like a place where you mostly lose.
VISUAL: Split screen. Left: Wick checking his phone every morning, weary; each red day arrives as a big thunderclap icon, each green day as a tiny sparkle. Right: the same account viewed once a year, a smooth line rising; Wick in a hammock, calm, glancing at it.
ON-SCREEN: Daily: terrifying. Yearly: calmer.

**B16** · `TEXT` · music: tension
VO: So in a crash, you are not really fighting the market. You are fighting a very old alarm. And that alarm only knows one word. Sell. But what actually happened to the people who stayed?
VISUAL: Marker writes on across the full screen: "You're fighting an old alarm." A flat red alarm bell bolted above the text starts ringing and shaking. Wick walks in from the right, determined, and calmly puts on a pair of earmuffs.
ON-SCREEN: You're fighting an old alarm.

## THE TURN

**B25** · `QUOTE` · music: resolve
VO: There is a line often credited to Warren Buffett, though nobody has found where he first said it. The stock market is a device for transferring money from the impatient to the patient.
VISUAL: A generic flat silhouette of an older man in glasses, captioned "often credited to Warren Buffett" with a small asterisk and "original source unknown" in fine print. The quote writes on in marker, one phrase at a time.
ON-SCREEN: "From the impatient to the patient."

**B26** · `MACHINE` · music: resolve
VO: Picture the machine. Fear pours in at the top. Scared owners feed their shares in at low prices. Out the other side come those same shares, into the hands of people who could sit still. The machine never forces anyone to sell. It just names a price and waits to see who flinches.
VISUAL: A big flat contraption of gears and pipes. A funnel on top is labeled FEAR. On the left, alarmed candles drop share certificates into a hopper labeled SELL. Gears turn, a conveyor belt carries the certificates right, and they drop into a basket held by a calm candle in a rocking chair labeled PATIENT. A price tag swings from the machine: SALE. Wick watches from the side, curious, head tilted.
ON-SCREEN: Every seller needs a buyer.

**B27** · `TEXT` · music: resolve
VO: So a crash is not only where prices fall. It is where shares change hands. For a broad fund, the loss becomes permanent at the moment you sell. Until then, it is a number and a feeling. Patience is not a personality trait. It is the price of admission.
VISUAL: Marker writes on across the full screen: "Patience is the price of admission." Wick walks in, calm, and holds up a paper ticket stub stamped PATIENCE. A small turnstile clicks him through.
ON-SCREEN: Patience is the price of admission.
