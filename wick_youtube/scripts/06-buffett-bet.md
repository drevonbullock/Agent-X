# Why Warren Buffett Bet a Million on a Robot

**Logline:** How Warren Buffett's ten year bet pitted a plain S&P 500 index fund against five hand picked funds of hedge funds, why the index fund won so clearly, and how fees quietly decide most of what you keep.

**The loop:** How did a fund with no brain at all beat some of the smartest money managers alive?

**The turn:** In investing, doing less beats being clever.

## CH4 · Why Smart Loses

**B18** · `CROWD` · music: wonder
VO: No. And this is the part that surprises people. Picture every investor in the stock market as one big crowd. Together, that crowd owns the whole market. So before costs, the average investor must earn exactly the market's return. Some beat it. Some trail it. The average is the market.
VISUAL: A huge crowd of tiny flat candles standing on one enormous flat pie chart labeled "The whole market." Some hold bigger slices up, some smaller. A giant average line sweeps across the crowd and settles exactly at the level labeled "Market return."
ON-SCREEN: Before costs, average = market
SFX: crowd murmur

**B19** · `FLOW` · music: tension
VO: Now take out the costs. Index funds cost very little, so they earn nearly the market's return. That means the active investors, the pickers, as a group, must earn less than the market after their fees. The economist William Sharpe laid this out in nineteen ninety one. It is not an opinion. It is arithmetic.
VISUAL: A flow diagram. "Market return" at the top splits into two pipes: "Index funds" with a tiny leak, and "Active pickers" with several big leaks labeled "fees" and "trading costs." The water level at the end of the active pipe is visibly lower. A small nameplate "William Sharpe, 1991" pins to the diagram.
ON-SCREEN: After costs, pickers trail. Math.

**B20** · `CHART_BAR` · music: tension
VO: The real world agrees. S and P Dow Jones Indices keeps a scorecard of professional stock funds. Over the fifteen years through the end of two thousand twenty five, about nine out of ten funds that picked large American stocks did worse than the S and P five hundred.
VISUAL: Ten flat fund icons in a row, each with a tiny bar. Nine bars stop short of a dashed line labeled "S&P 500." One bar pokes above it and does a small victory dance. The counter above reads 89.93%.
ON-SCREEN: 15 years: ~90% trailed the index
SFX: pop

**B21** · `STAGE` · music: tension
VO: Some managers do win. But you have to pick them in advance, and picking the winning picker is its own bet. That is the trap. The problem is not that the professionals are dumb. It is that being clever costs money, every year, whether it works or not.
VISUAL: A flat carnival shell game. Ten cups on a table, one hides a golden coin. A barker candle shuffles them fast. Wick stands at the table with a single coin to bet, expression weary. Background gag: a sign on the booth reads "Entry fee charged whether you win or not."
ON-SCREEN: Clever costs. Win or lose.

## CH5 · The Slow Leak

**B22** · `MACHINE` · music: tension
VO: Here is how that cost reaches you. Every fund charges an expense ratio. That is a yearly fee, written as a percent of your money, taken quietly from inside the fund. You never get a bill. Your balance just grows a little slower than it would have.
VISUAL: A flat glass jar of coins sits on a shelf labeled "Your fund." A tiny faucet near the bottom drips one coin at a time into a funnel labeled "Expense ratio." There is no receipt, no alert. Wick taps the jar, expression curious, and does not notice the drip.
ON-SCREEN: Expense ratio: a fee you never see
SFX: tick

**B23** · `COUNTER` · music: tension
VO: Let's put numbers on the leak. Say you invest ten thousand dollars once and leave it for thirty years. Say it grows seven percent a year before fees. That rate is illustrative, not a promise. With no fee at all, you end up with about seventy six thousand dollars.
VISUAL: A large counter rolls from $10,000 to $76,123 as a year ticker runs from 1 to 30. A small label under the counter reads "Illustrative: 7% a year, 30 years."
ON-SCREEN: $10,000 at 7% for 30 years
SFX: tick, tick, chime

**B24** · `SPLIT` · music: tension

With a fee of one percent, you end up with about fifty seven thousand. That one percent took almost eighteen thousand dollars. Nearly a quarter of everything.
VISUAL: Split. Left jar labeled "0.05% fee" fills to $75,063. Right jar labeled "1% fee" fills to $57,435. A dashed line marks the gap and a bracket reads "$17,628." The right jar's faucet has been dripping the whole time. Wick stares at the gap, expression alarmed.
ON-SCREEN: 0.05%: $75,063. 1%: $57,435.
SFX: thud

**B25** · `QUOTE` · music: wonder
VO: One percent sounds tiny. But it is taken every year, from your gains and from your gains on your gains. Buffett put it in seven words. Performance comes, performance goes. Fees never falter.
VISUAL: The Buffett silhouette, now at a desk writing a letter by lamplight. The quote writes on in marker, beat by beat. On "Fees never falter," the jar's faucet from B22 keeps dripping in the corner, steady as a clock.
ON-SCREEN: "Fees never falter."

## PAYOFF

**B29** · `STAGE` · music: resolve
VO: So how did a robot with no brain beat some of the smartest money on Wall Street? It did not need to be smart. It only needed to be cheap, and to sit still. The professionals had to be right by enough to pay for all their cleverness. In this bet, not one of the five was.
VISUAL: Callback to B01. The charity's front room. The long check is now pinned up on the wall. Beside it, a framed photo of the sleeping robot with the trophy. Wick stands in front of it, arms folded, expression proud.

**B30** · `SPLIT` · music: resolve
VO: One honest warning. The robot does not protect you from crashes. When the market falls, an index fund falls with it, like it did in two thousand eight. What it protects you from is paying, year after year, for promises that usually don't come true.
VISUAL: Split. Left: the robot riding the 2008 bar down like a slide again, calm, holding on. Right: a flat salesperson candle with a glossy brochure labeled "We'll beat the market!", the brochure slowly fading. Wick between them, expression calm.
ON-SCREEN: Falls with the market. Skips the promises.
