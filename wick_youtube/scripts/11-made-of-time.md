# Your Money Is Made of Your Life

**Logline:** How to translate any price from dollars into hours of your life, using your real hourly wage, and why a Roman philosopher thought that translation was the most important math there is.

**The loop:** A price tag says $180. What is the real price, and why can't we see it?

**The turn:** You don't spend money. You spend the hours it cost.

## CH2 · The Wage on Paper

**B10** · `QUOTE` · music: warm

**B11** · `STAGE` · music: warm

**B13** · `FLOW` · music: warm

## CH3 · The Hidden Hours

**B15** · `CHART_BAR` · music: warm
VO: Now the hours. Forty on the clock. But he also has to get there. The Census Bureau puts the average American commute at about twenty seven minutes each way. Twice a day, five days a week, that is four and a half hours.
VISUAL: A bar builds up like a stack of blocks. A big block lands: "40 hrs on the clock". A smaller block drops on top with a thud: "4.5 hrs commuting". A tiny flat car drives along the top edge of the new block, brake lights blinking in traffic.
ON-SCREEN: Average commute: 27 min each way

**B16** · `CHART_BAR` · music: warm
VO: Then there is time that belongs to the job but is never paid. Getting ready. The unpaid lunch break. The evening on the couch, too drained to do anything else. Say that is five and a half more hours. Wick's real work week is fifty hours, not forty.
VISUAL: Three thin blocks land on the bar one after another: "getting ready", "unpaid lunch", "recovering". The bar now reaches a dashed line labeled 50. Wick stands next to it, weary, craning his neck to see the top.
ON-SCREEN: Real work week: 50 hours

VISUAL: A big long division in orange marker: "$600 ÷ 50 hrs". A counter above it spins down from $25 and locks on $12 with a heavy thud. Wick, alarmed, eyes wide, drops his pencil. It bounces twice.
ON-SCREEN: Real hourly wage: $12

**B18** · `TEXT` · music: wonder

## CH4 · Prices in Hours

**B19** · `SPLIT` · music: wonder
VO: At his paper wage, Wick's brain priced the sneakers at about seven hours. At his real wage, one hundred and eighty dollars divided by twelve is fifteen hours. A day and a half of his working life, for shoes.
VISUAL: Split screen. Left tag in soft grey: "$180 ÷ $25 = 7.2 hrs". Right tag in bold amber: "$180 ÷ $12 = 15 hrs". Wick stands in the middle holding one sneaker in each hand, curious, weighing them like a scale. The right one sinks lower.
ON-SCREEN: $180 = 15 hours of life

**B20** · `COUNTER` · music: tension
VO: Now something bigger. Say he is looking at a car with a payment of five hundred and fifty dollars a month. Divide by twelve. That is about forty six hours of his life. Every month. Nearly a full real work week, just to keep the car in the driveway.
VISUAL: A shiny flat car in a driveway. Above it a counter climbs from 0 to 46 hours. A wall calendar flips to the next month; the counter resets to 0 and climbs to 46 again; the calendar flips again. Wick sits in the driver's seat, sombre, hands on the wheel, not going anywhere.
ON-SCREEN: $550/month = 46 hours/month
SFX: tick

**B21** · `STAGE` · music: warm
VO: This is not a rule against buying things. Some things are worth every hour. A trip with people you love. A tool that grows your skills. The point is to see the trade clearly. Is this worth fifteen hours of my life? Sometimes the honest answer is yes.
VISUAL: Wick at a fork in a path. One path leads to a heap of unopened shopping boxes gathering dust; the other leads to a campfire where a few candle friends wave him over. He picks the campfire, happy. Background gag: one box in the dusty heap wears a tiny cobweb and a sticker reading "STILL IN PLASTIC".
ON-SCREEN: Worth the hours? Sometimes yes.

**B22** · `STACK` · music: warm
VO: And it works in reverse. Money you do not spend is stored time. Say Wick saves six thousand dollars. At twelve dollars an hour, that is five hundred hours. Ten of his real work weeks, already paid for, waiting for him. Which raises the real question Seneca was asking. How many hours are there in all?
VISUAL: Coins stack into a tower, and as each coin lands it flips into a tiny hourglass. A label on the tower reads "$6,000 = 500 hours". Ten small calendar pages marked "WEEK" peel off the tower and float up like kites. Wick, proud, leans against the tower with his arms crossed.
ON-SCREEN: $6,000 saved = 500 hours back

## THE TURN

**B23** · `ZOOM` · music: resolve
VO: In the United States, life expectancy is now about seventy nine years. That is roughly four thousand one hundred weeks. The writer Oliver Burkeman put it bluntly. The average human lifespan is absurdly, terrifyingly, insultingly short. Every purchase comes out of that pile.

ON-SCREEN: 79 years ≈ 4,100 weeks

**B24** · `TEXT` · music: resolve

VISUAL: Marker writes on, big: "You don't spend money. You spend the hours it cost." Then smaller underneath: "Money is the receipt." Wick walks in, calm, holding a paper receipt that unrolls down to the floor, printed not with prices but with little clock faces.

**B25** · `FLOW` · music: resolve
VO: Seen that way, a raise is not just more money. It is fewer hours for every thing you buy. A debt is not just a balance. It is hours you have already promised away. And savings are hours you have taken back.
VISUAL: A flow diagram with Wick's hourglass at the center. Arrow one: a RAISE box, and the hourglass tags on nearby items shrink. Arrow two: a DEBT box, and a chain of hour tokens marches out of the hourglass toward a door labeled LENDER. Arrow three: a SAVINGS box, and tokens stream back into Wick's pocket. Wick, determined, catches the last one.
ON-SCREEN: Debt = hours promised away

**B26** · `QUOTE` · music: resolve
VO: This is what Seneca meant. Not that money is bad. He plainly liked it. But money can be earned again. The hour you traded for it cannot. So he told a man who spent his days counting grain to start counting his days.
VISUAL: Back in the Roman warehouse. Paulinus, the toga candle, sets down his abacus mid click. He turns to a small sundial on the windowsill and watches the shadow move. Seneca's silhouette stands in the doorway.
ON-SCREEN: Money comes back. Hours don't.
