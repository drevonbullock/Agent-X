# Every Empire Goes Broke the Same Way

**Logline:** By the end, the viewer understands Ray Dalio's framework for how reserve currency empires rise and fade (the Dutch guilder, the British pound, the US dollar), the long term debt cycle underneath it, and how the exact same arc plays out inside one household, plus the one number that shows where their own household sits.

**The loop:** In the seventeen eighties, people in Amsterdam rushed to swap the world's most trusted money for gold. Why do the richest powers on earth keep losing their money the same way, and what does that have to do with your kitchen table?

**The turn:** Empires and households fall the same way.

**Tone guardrails:** Non-partisan and non-doom. No predictions about any country, party or date. The framework is Ray Dalio's and is credited on screen every time it's used. History is told as mechanism, not as a warning siren.

## CH2 · Borrowing Tomorrow

**B09** · `FLOW` · music: tension
VO: Dalio's answer starts with debt. When you borrow, you spend money today that you haven't earned yet. In his short film, How the Economic Machine Works, he puts it plainly. Every time you borrow, you create a cycle.
VISUAL: Flow diagram: a box labeled "Future you" hands a coin backward along an arrow to a box labeled "Today you". Today you spends it at a flat market stall. A second arrow curls from the stall back around to Future you, labeled "payments". The loop glows. Wick appears in both boxes: expression happy as Today you, expression weary as Future you.
ON-SCREEN: Every borrow starts a cycle. (Dalio)
SFX: whoosh

**B10** · `CHART_LINE` · music: tension
VO: Here's why. Borrowing pulls spending forward. You get more now, so you'll have less later, when the payments come due. Borrow for something that pays you back, like training or a business, and the cycle can be healthy. Borrow to live beyond what you produce, and the bill just waits.
VISUAL: A spending line rises above a dashed "income" line, then dips below it later, the two gaps shaded the same size: the hump labeled "now", the dip labeled "later". Then a split: a small branch where the borrowed coin plants a tree that grows fruit (healthy), and another branch where it buys a party hat that deflates (the bill waits, drawn as a tiny envelope tapping its foot).
ON-SCREEN: Borrowing pulls spending forward.

**B11** · `CHART_LINE` · music: tension
VO: Dalio describes two debt cycles. A short one, about five to eight years, that we feel as booms and recessions. And a long one, about seventy five to a hundred years. Each short cycle tends to leave a little more debt behind than the last, until the long one peaks.
VISUAL: Small waves draw left to right, each wave's low point sitting slightly higher than the one before, while a faint upper line labeled "total debt" climbs steadily behind them. The small waves are labeled "5 to 8 years"; the long climb is labeled "75 to 100 years". At the far right, the big line crests.
ON-SCREEN: Two debt cycles: short and long

**B12** · `MACHINE` · music: tension
VO: At the peak, payments on old debts eat so much income that there's little left to spend. Lenders get nervous. Borrowing gets harder. And a country that issues the world's money faces a tempting shortcut. Create more money to cover the bills. So what happens when it does?
VISUAL: A flat machine: coins drop into a hopper labeled "Income", but a big gear labeled "Old debt payments" grinds most of them into a chute before they reach the spout labeled "Spending". Only a trickle comes out. On the side of the machine, a red lever labeled "PRINT" glows. A mitten hovers over it.
ON-SCREEN: The tempting shortcut
SFX: gears, then tick

## THE TURN

## FIRST STEP + CLOSE

**B31** · `TEXT` · music: resolve
VO: So here's your first step. Calculate your debt to income ratio. Add up all your monthly debt payments. Use the monthly payments, not the total balances. Then divide by your monthly income before taxes. That's the whole formula.
VISUAL: Marker write on as a fraction: numerator "Monthly debt payments", denominator "Monthly income before taxes", equals "Your DTI". Wick holds a flat calculator, expression focused.
ON-SCREEN: Debt payments ÷ gross income

**B32** · `COUNTER` · music: resolve
VO: The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau gives an example. Two thousand dollars a month in debt payments, on six thousand dollars a month of income, is thirty three percent. Write your number down. If it climbs over time, your debt is growing faster than your income.
VISUAL: A counter shows "$2,000 ÷ $6,000" and rolls to "33%". Then a small sticky note with "__%" on it flies onto a flat fridge door and sticks. A tiny arrow beside it points up with a warning tag: "rising? debt is outrunning income".
ON-SCREEN: $2,000 ÷ $6,000 = 33%
SFX: tick, then pop
